colorizing the prokudin-gorskii photo collection

project 1 · sep 15, 2026

turning three black and white exposures of the same scene, taken through red, green, and blue filters, into one color photo by lining them up.

the data

sergei prokudin-gorskii photographed the russian empire in color starting in 1907 by taking three exposures of every scene on one glass plate, each through a different color filter. the library of congress scanned the plates and put them online. each scan is a tall image with the blue exposure on top, then green, then red.

the first step is to cut the plate into three equal parts. i read the image, convert it to floats in [0, 1] so jpgs and 16-bit tifs behave the same, and slice by thirds along the height. 

img = sk.img_as_float(skio.imread(path))

height = img.shape[0] // 3
b = img[:height]
g = img[height:2 * height]
r = img[2 * height:3 * height]

if you just stack the three parts as is you get a color image, but the three exposures were not taken from exactly the same spot on the plate, so nothing lines up.

part 1 · single-scale alignment

i keep the blue channel fixed and move the green and red channels to match it. the model is a plain x, y translation. to find the right one i try every shift in a window of [-15, 15] pixels in both directions, score how well the shifted channel matches blue, and keep the best.

the score needs some way to say how similar two images are. i tried two. the first is the l2 norm, which is just the sum of squared differences between the two images. smaller is better. 

def l2(a, b):
    return float(np.sum((a - b) ** 2))

the second is normalized cross-correlation. you subtract the mean from each image, divide each by its norm, and take the dot product. this ignores overall brightness and contrast differences and only looks at whether the two images go up and down in the same places. a perfect match gives 1, so i negate it to keep smaller is better like l2. 

def ncc(a, b):
    a = a - a.mean()
    b = b - b.mean()
    return float(-np.sum(a * b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)))

one thing that matters a lot is ignoring the borders. the scans have black and white bars around the edges, and those bars are in different places on each channel. if you score the whole image the bars dominate and the search locks onto them instead of the picture. so before scoring i crop 10% off every side of both images. 

def crop(img, frac=0.1):
    h, w = img.shape
    dh, dw = int(h * frac), int(w * frac)
    return img[dh:h - dh, dw:w - dw]

the search is two nested loops over the window. for each shift i roll the moving channel with np.roll, crop both, and score. the crop only happens for scoring. the final image uses the full channel rolled by whatever shift wins. 

def search(moving, fixed, metric, center=(0, 0), radius=15):
    fixed = crop(fixed)
    best_shift, best_score = center, np.inf
    for dy in range(center[0] - radius, center[0] + radius + 1):
        for dx in range(center[1] - radius, center[1] + radius + 1):
            score = metric(crop(np.roll(moving, (dy, dx), axis=(0, 1))), fixed)
            if score < best_score:
                best_shift, best_score = (dy, dx), score
    return best_shift

here are the three small jpgs aligned this way. the offsets are (dy, dx) in pixels. l2 and ncc pick the exact same shifts on all three.

part 2 · image pyramid

the full size tifs are about 3200 by 3700 pixels per channel, roughly ten times bigger than the jpgs in each direction. that breaks the single-scale search in two ways. the real shifts are now up to 178 pixels, so a [-15, 15] window can't even reach them. and scoring one shift at that size takes about 20ms, so a wide enough window would take many minutes per image.

the fix is to align at a small scale first and then refine. i build an image pyramid by repeatedly halving the image. before dropping every other pixel i blur with a gaussian so the small image doesn't alias. i keep halving until the next level would be under 200 pixels on its short side, which gives 5 levels for the tifs and 2 for the jpgs. 

def downsample(img):
    return gaussian(img, sigma=1)[::2, ::2]

def build_pyramid(img, min_size=200):
    levels = [img]
    while min(levels[-1].shape) // 2 >= min_size:
        levels.append(downsample(levels[-1]))
    return levels

then i run the same search from part 1 at the coarsest level with the full [-15, 15] window. at that scale 15 pixels is 240 pixels at full resolution, so everything is in reach. the answer i get is only good to about a pixel at that scale. moving one level finer, every pixel becomes two, so i double the shift and search a small window of [-3, 3] around it to fix the rounding. i repeat that down to full resolution. 

def align_pyramid(moving, fixed, metric, radius=15, refine_radius=3):
    moving_pyr = build_pyramid(moving)
    fixed_pyr = build_pyramid(fixed)

    shift = search(moving_pyr[-1], fixed_pyr[-1], metric, radius=radius)
    for mov, fix in zip(moving_pyr[-2::-1], fixed_pyr[-2::-1]):
        shift = (2 * shift[0], 2 * shift[1])
        shift = search(mov, fix, metric, center=shift, radius=refine_radius)
    return shift

the small refine window is what makes this fast. the expensive level is the full resolution one, and there i only score 49 shifts instead of 961. each tif takes about 4 seconds with ncc and the whole set of 17 runs in about a minute.

l2 vs ncc

on the jpgs the two metrics agree exactly. on the tifs they mostly agree, but l2 gets church wrong and ncc gets it right. the red channel of church is brighter overall than the blue, and l2 is sensitive to that, so it finds a shift that matches overall brightness instead of the actual content. ncc subtracts the mean first so it doesn't care.

where it fails

emir doesn't align with either metric. his robe is bright blue, so it comes out very bright in the blue exposure and very dark in the red one. the two channels really do look different, not just shifted, and neither l2 nor ncc can match them since both compare raw pixel values. the green channel is close enough to blue that it still lines up. the red channel ends up far off.

the fix would be to align on something other than brightness, like edges or gradients, since the outline of the robe is in the same place in every channel even if the fill isn't.
